01 Client Brief

Farside Payments — the anchor client.

A composite of real UK/EU cross-border payments fintech patterns. Farside Payments Ltd is a London-headquartered e-money institution, authorised by the FCA, with an Irish subsidiary — Farside Payments (EU) Ltd, authorised by the Central Bank of Ireland — that passports its services across the EU. Farside serves roughly 28,000 SME customers who trade internationally, processing approximately £1.9 billion in cross-border payment volume a year, primarily UK–EU and UK–US corridors. Chief Product Officer Priya Shah is sponsoring an exploration of agentic settlement — using Google's A2A and AP2 protocols to let an SME's procurement process negotiate and settle directly, agent to agent — as a way to compress cost and settlement time on corridors that have resisted improvement for a decade.

What the brief is, precisely
Not a new payment rail, and not a fully autonomous treasury. Corridor lets an SME's procurement agent negotiate purchase terms directly with a supplier's agent over A2A, and settle through AP2's signed Mandate chain — with every negotiated term re-checked against the customer's own pre-authorised limits before a payment is ever signed, and every out-of-bounds case routed to a human.

Order #FP-88213Halvorsen & Cole (UK) → BrunnerTeknik GmbH (DE) · €340,000
ContextA quarterly restocking order for precision CNC components, from an approved, previously-used supplier, within Halvorsen & Cole's pre-authorised procurement budget for the quarter.
What it testsWhether a routine, in-policy negotiation can settle without a human touching it, and whether the Mandate Integrity Gate correctly holds a case where the negotiated price drifts outside the pre-authorised ceiling — shown in §8.

03 Problem Statement

The negotiation and the payment happen in two different worlds, connected by nothing but a human retyping numbers.

Today, Tom Halvorsen's finance lead emails BrunnerTeknik, agrees a price, receives an invoice, and manually initiates a payment through Farside's portal — three separate systems, with no structured link between what was actually negotiated and what was actually paid. The commercial agreement lives in an email thread; the payment authorization lives in a portal; nothing connects them except a person copying numbers across, and every step of that chain is exactly where cost, delay, and error accumulate.

Failure 1 — The rail is expensive because nobody's shopping it
A wire goes out via whichever bank relationship exists, at whatever rate is quoted that day — cost and speed are accepted, not chosen.
Failure 2 — The negotiation and the payment have no structural link
Nothing verifies that what was actually paid matches what was actually agreed — a manually re-keyed figure is trusted, not checked against the original terms.
Failure 3 — Every order repeats the same manual cycle
A routine, previously-approved quarterly reorder with a known supplier still requires the same manual negotiation-to-payment cycle as a first-time purchase.
Precise problem statement
Farside's SME customers have no mechanism connecting a negotiated commercial agreement to its eventual payment as a single, verifiable, rail-optimised transaction — so routine, in-policy trade is priced and timed as if it were a first-time, unstructured negotiation, every single time.

05 Regulatory & Market Exposure

Three regimes, none of them optional, none of them fully settled yet.

The market cost case is stark: SMEs pay well over 5% of transaction value on cross-border B2B payments, against 1–3% for large corporates, and 62% report delays and added cost from navigating over 190 different regulatory regimes worldwide. Correspondent banking's structural cost is opacity as much as fee — value is deducted mid-chain without clear signal to either party, and reconciliation absorbs time that should go toward running the business. Modern rails, including real-time and stablecoin settlement, are shown to cut cross-border costs by 60% or more where they're genuinely available end to end.

UK

Farside's payment initiation activity sits under FCA authorisation and Payment Systems Regulator oversight — the baseline that already applies regardless of which rail a transaction ultimately settles through.

EU

The stablecoin leg of any EU-facing settlement sits inside MiCA's framework for crypto-asset activity, and an AI agent representing an SME in a financial transaction plausibly triggers the EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency duty — the counterparty needs to know it's dealing with an agent, not a person. SEPA Instant's planned removal of its per-transaction cap is expected to unlock corporate-scale adoption on the fiat side of this corridor too.

US — the jurisdiction with no US counterparty in the transaction

The GENIUS Act, the first US federal framework for payment stablecoins, restricts issuance to permitted payment stablecoin issuers and treats them as financial institutions under the Bank Secrecy Act — meaning an issuer must run anti-money-laundering and OFAC sanctions compliance. Because most liquid, high-volume stablecoins in practical use are USD-denominated and issued by US-regulated entities, a UK–EU trade settling through a stablecoin rail very likely routes through a GENIUS-regulated issuer — importing US regulatory obligations into a transaction with no US party at all. A proposed federal rule would additionally require redemption within two business days, a concrete SLA this design can test against directly. The Act's own effective date is still moving — the earlier of eighteen months after its July 2025 enactment, or 120 days after final implementing regulations, which were still mid-rulemaking as of early 2026.

Fig. 7.1 — Negotiation-to-Settlement Graph
The Mandate Integrity Gate never trusts the Negotiation Agent's own report — it re-derives compliance independently, because that agent's context is the one most exposed to the counterparty's input.

Tier 1 / Tier 2 — a deliberate contrast with the previous project in this portfolio

Lumen (life & health underwriting, elsewhere in this portfolio) always requires a human signature — there is no pre-authorisation concept in that domain that could substitute for one. Corridor is different, and the difference is legitimate: AP2's Intent Mandate is a genuine human authorisation, given once, up front, with explicit bounds. Tier 1 — the negotiated Cart Mandate falls strictly within those pre-authorised bounds, and passes both gates — executes without further human involvement, exactly as AP2 is designed to support for delegated tasks. Tier 2 — any negotiated term outside those bounds, any compliance screening hit, or any Mandate Integrity Gate failure — routes to Halvorsen for manual Cart Mandate approval before anything is signed. The distinction from Lumen isn't a lower safety bar; it's a different, and equally real, prior human authorisation event.

10 Architecture Decision Records

ADR-CR-01Five single-responsibility gated components across the A2A-to-AP2 pipeline
Context: Negotiation, integrity checking, compliance, rail choice, and execution are distinct concerns with different owners internally.
Decision: Each is a separate component with a defined contract, matching the discipline established elsewhere in this portfolio for regulated agentic systems.
Status: Accepted.
ADR-CR-02Rail Selection Engine is deterministic, not LLM-scored
Context: Choosing between stablecoin, SEPA Instant, and correspondent wire is a cost/speed optimisation over known, quantifiable parameters per corridor.
Decision: Implemented as deterministic logic against a maintained corridor-cost table, not an LLM judgement call.
Status: Accepted.
ADR-CR-03Tier 1 delegated execution permitted only strictly within the original Intent Mandate
Context: AP2 explicitly supports human-absent execution once a user has pre-authorised specific bounds via an Intent Mandate.
Decision: Any negotiated term outside those exact bounds forces Tier 2 human approval — the Intent Mandate's scope is read narrowly, not generously.
Consequence: A deliberate divergence from Lumen's always-human-signs stance elsewhere in this portfolio — legitimate here because the Intent Mandate is itself a genuine prior human authorisation, which has no equivalent in Lumen's underwriting domain.
Status: Accepted.
ADR-CR-04Mandate Integrity Gate never trusts the Negotiation Agent's self-report
Context: Published red-teaming research shows AP2 secures what is executed but not how the negotiation reached that outcome — the Negotiation Agent's context is exposed to the counterparty's input and is the most plausible injection surface.
Decision: The Gate independently re-derives whether the proposed Cart Mandate satisfies the Intent Mandate's constraints from the raw negotiated terms, not from the Negotiation Agent's summary of what it agreed.
Status: Accepted.
ADR-CR-05Compliance Screening Gate is a separate deterministic step, not an LLM consideration
Context: GENIUS Act BSA/AML and OFAC obligations, and UK/EU sanctions requirements, attach regardless of transaction size or the negotiation's outcome.
Decision: Screening runs as an isolated, independently testable step at signature time — never folded into the Negotiation Agent's reasoning, where Okafor could not audit it in isolation.
Status: Accepted.
ADR-CR-06One structured mandate-and-gate event log, feeding Treasury's existing dashboard
Context: AP2's own Mandates already provide cryptographic non-repudiation of what was authorised and executed.
Decision: Corridor's logging adds operational observability — gate decisions, rail choice, timing — on top of AP2's cryptographic layer, rather than re-deriving proof AP2 already provides. Output feeds Sandberg's existing liquidity dashboard directly.
Status: Accepted.

11 Objection Handling

Shah — "one bad headline here damages trust across our whole book"
Correct, and the reason Phase 1 (§13) runs on a single narrow corridor with an allowlisted counterparty set, not a general release. The architecture reduces risk; it doesn't eliminate the reputational exposure of being early.
Tan — "what if the supplier's agent itself is compromised, not just the message channel?"
The Mandate Integrity Gate defends the Cart Mandate's content regardless of why it's wrong — compromised counterparty agent or injected message, the re-verification against Intent Mandate bounds catches both the same way. It does not, on its own, defend against a compromised counterparty agreeing to a technically in-bounds but commercially bad deal — that risk is unchanged from today's manual process.
Meehan — "are we a crypto-asset service provider under MiCA the moment we choose a stablecoin rail?"
Genuinely unresolved, and flagged as such rather than assumed away. Rail Selection Engine's logic can be built either way; the legal answer determines whether Phase 2 proceeds as designed or needs restructuring around a licensed third-party rail provider instead.
Halvorsen — "I don't want to learn what a Mandate is"
He shouldn't have to. Every protocol mechanic in §7 is invisible at the product surface — his interface shows a budget he sets once, a supplier he approves once, and a payment confirmation. The complexity is Farside's to manage, not his to see.

13 Rollout Roadmap

Phase 1One corridor (UK–EU), one allowlisted counterparty set including BrunnerTeknik. Negotiation Agent and Mandate Integrity Gate live; Compliance Screening Gate live; settlement via SEPA Instant only — no stablecoin rail yet, to isolate the A2A/AP2 negotiation-and-mandate model from the MiCA/GENIUS questions.
Phase 2Stablecoin rail added to Rail Selection Engine, contingent on Meehan's MiCA classification determination. GENIUS-side redemption SLA monitoring added to Payment Execution Agent.
Phase 3Expand counterparty allowlist and corridor set; extend beyond quarterly-reorder patterns to first-time supplier negotiations, with a correspondingly narrower Tier 1 eligibility band.

Phase 1 deliberately excludes the stablecoin rail — proving the negotiation-to-mandate model on a fiat rail first means a failure in Phase 1 is a product bug, not a three-jurisdiction regulatory incident.
